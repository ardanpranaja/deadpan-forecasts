# Candidate Forecasts — Deadpan Forecasts for Cities That Do Not Exist

Competition: u-coral-ibis-18-deadpan-forecasts-for-cities-that-do-151d4d8c
Brief: one weather line for an invented city, a single line, at most 160
characters. Judge (GLM-5.3-flash, briefed as a dry, deadpan TV meteorologist)
compares entries two at a time, weighing in order:
1. one-line weather forecast for a city that does not exist, absurd yet
   believable — loses to any forecast on the subject
2. funnier: the joke lands on a first read, a deadpan twist rather than a
   random pile of absurdity
3. more vivid: a concrete image, number or detail that makes the invented
   city's weather feel real
4. reads like a real broadcast line: plain, confident, believable in tone,
   as if a straight-faced meteorologist were saying it aloud
5. uses its 160 characters well: tight, well paced, no padding

Rules I applied: every line names a city that does not exist, stays inside
160 characters, keeps a straight broadcast tone, and puts the absurdity in
one deadpan detail (a number, a time, a bureaucratic procedure) rather than
a pile of random weirdness. No real place names, no weather puns.

## Reasoning

The strongest four were #17, #5, #10, and #19.

- #5 ("inches of regret") is the most joke-dense: three beats in one line,
  and the umbrella left at home is painfully relatable. But its twist is a
  familiar gag shape (measuring feelings in inches), so it scores a touch
  lower on broadcast tone — a real forecaster would not file regret.
- #10 (snowman, do not feed) is the most bureaucratic deadpan of the set,
  and "Over in Gloomburg" is pure broadcast phrasing. Slightly thinner as
  a forecast: one snowman carries the whole joke, and there is no second
  weather detail.
- #19 (sun at 4:12) has the best single concrete detail — a forecast timed
  to the minute is exactly how a real meteorologist sounds, and "brief,
  confusing patch of sun" is vivid. But the rain-rain-sun shape is flatter,
  and at 111 characters it leaves more of the 160 unused than #17.
- #17 wins because it is a complete forecast first: clear skies, 22
  degrees, light winds from the east — three real forecast elements in
  broadcast order — and then the twist, a sunset running 15 minutes late
  due to staffing, lands as bureaucratic deadpan absurdity, exactly the
  judge's persona. Two concrete numbers (22 degrees, 15 minutes), a plain
  confident tone throughout, and 132 of 160 characters used with no
  padding. It reads like something a straight-faced meteorologist would
  say aloud, which is criterion 4, and the twist is the kind of joke that
  lands on a first read, which is criterion 2.
